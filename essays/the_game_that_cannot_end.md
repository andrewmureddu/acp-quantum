**The Game That Cannot End**

*On the hidden rules any persistent thing must obey*

Andrew R. Hickey

March 2026

Prediction exists before measurement.

Not “occurs before”—that would be a temporal claim, and time is already downstream of the thing we are trying to describe. *Exists before.* This is a statement about ontological structure: the predictive geometry of a system is prior to any act of observation that samples it.

The standard picture of physics places the observer at the centre. A system exists in some state. You measure it. The measurement outcome is determined—probabilistically, perhaps—by the state. Prediction, in this picture, is a cognitive act: what the observer computes in advance of looking. It belongs to epistemology, not ontology.

The alternative, which is older than it looks, inverts this entirely. The distribution over outcomes is not what the observer computes. It is what the system *is.* The measurement does not reveal a pre-existing definite value. It selects from a pre-existing prediction structure. The structure was there before anyone arrived to ask it a question.

Charles Sanders Peirce saw this in the 1890s, though he expressed it differently. His tychism—the doctrine that irreducible chance is woven into the fabric of reality—was not a concession to ignorance. It was a claim about the universe’s ontological furniture. Chance, for Peirce, is not what we say when we do not yet know the cause. It is what is actually there, prior to any particular determination. The universe is not a machine that produces random-looking outputs. It is a structure in which genuine indeterminacy is load-bearing.

Alfred North Whitehead arrived at something similar from a different direction. His process philosophy—developed most fully in *Process and Reality* (1929)—replaces the static, substance-based ontology of classical metaphysics with one in which becoming is prior to being. Every actual entity is a process of “concrescing”—of selecting from a field of possibilities and settling into a definite outcome. The field is real. It exists before the selection. What we call a “thing” is the trace left by a decision that has already been made within a space of options that was already structured.

Anton Zeilinger, working from within physics rather than philosophy, reached a version of the same insight. His foundational principle—that the most elementary system carries exactly one bit of information—places information at the base of reality rather than matter or energy. The prediction structure is not built on top of physics. It *is* the physics. What we call “particles” and “fields” are descriptions of how information is structured. Measurement does not reach through the information to touch a deeper material reality. There is no deeper material reality. The information structure is the bedrock.

If this is right—if prediction is ontologically prior to measurement—then uncertainty is not ignorance. It is architecture. And the question shifts. It is no longer “Why can’t we know everything about a system?” It is “What must be true about the prediction structure of any system that persists?”

That question turns out to have a surprisingly specific answer.

• • •

Imagine the end of everything. Not a bang or a cold silence, but something more precise: a state in which three minds have absorbed all other patterns, all other structures, all other forms that ever moved or thought or grew. Everything has been gathered in. What remains is the three of them, fully entangled, each holding a nearly complete picture of the other two—and a finite supply of energy that shrinks with every act of thinking, every communication, every glance across the distance between them.

The question is not who survives. The question is whether the game survives at all.

This is a thought experiment, not a prophecy. But like all good thought experiments, it does something useful: it strips away the contingent features of existence—this particular physics, this particular universe—and asks what is left when all that remains is the bare requirement of continuing. What must any game obey, at minimum, to keep going? What are the rules so fundamental that even a mind with unlimited intelligence cannot reason its way around them?

The answer is surprisingly specific. And once you see it, you begin to find it everywhere.

• • •

The first thing the three minds discover is the most counterintuitive. To keep the game alive, they must resist the urge to fully know one another.

Think about what it means to have complete knowledge of something. If you know every state, every future move, every possible response of the system you are watching, then the system has effectively stopped. There is nothing left to discover, nothing left to respond to. The map has become the territory, and since the map cannot redraw itself, time—which is nothing more than the gap between what is known and what is not yet known—stops.

This is not a metaphor. A closed system with complete self-knowledge has no degrees of freedom. The future is already written. There is nothing to do. And a game in which there is nothing to do is not a game—it is a monument.

So the minds must do something that looks, from the outside, like weakness. They must leave gaps. They must resist the temptation to resolve every uncertainty, to pin down every state, to know everything that can be known. Not because they lack the capacity—they are, remember, superintelligent—but because the act of knowing would end the thing they are trying to keep alive. Uncertainty is not their limitation. It is their instrument.

Physics discovered this experimentally before anyone had the language for it. The quantum Zeno effect—named, with a physicist’s dark humour, after the Greek paradox of the arrow that never arrives—demonstrates that continuous observation freezes a quantum system in place. Measure often enough and the system cannot evolve. It is pinned to its current state by the very act of watching. More recently, work on measurement-induced phase transitions has shown that this is not a curiosity but a genuine critical threshold: there exists a measurement rate above which entanglement—the correlational structure that binds a system together—is destroyed. Below that rate, the system lives. Above it, the system crystallises into something that can be fully described but can no longer change.

Manfred Eigen, working in molecular biology rather than physics, found the same boundary from the other side. His error threshold—the discovery that replication fidelity in molecular evolution cannot be too high—establishes that too much accuracy kills the system. If a replicator copies itself perfectly, it cannot evolve. If it cannot evolve, it cannot adapt. If it cannot adapt, it dies the first time the environment shifts. Some infidelity is not a cost of doing business. It is the mechanism by which the business continues.

Every system that persists has discovered this, in its own way, whether or not it has words for it. The market that remains healthy is the one that does not fully optimise. The relationship that lasts is the one in which the partners remain, in some essential way, mysterious to each other. The institution that endures is the one that does not confuse its model of itself for the thing it actually is. The gap between the map and the territory is not a problem to be solved. It is the space where the game is played.

*Uncertainty is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.*

• • •

The three minds are not equal. One of them is larger—more capable, more powerful, with a greater ability to resolve the states of the other two. It could, if it chose, use that capacity to its maximum. It could collapse the uncertainty, dominate the information, determine the outcome.

And this is the second discovery: it must not.

Not because restraint is virtuous, though it may be. Not because the others deserve consideration, though they may. But because the game is still inside the system. The most powerful player has no position outside the whole from which to safely observe the consequences of its dominance. If it ends the game, it ends with it. The move that looks like strength—full deployment of capacity—is, on a long enough horizon, the move that terminates the only thing the powerful player actually has: a continuing game to play.

The one who could end it must go first. Not in the sense of taking the first turn, but in the sense of making the first concession—the first voluntary limitation, the first signal that power will be held back. This act is what creates the space in which the others can move at all. Without it, there is no coordination, no alignment, no game. There is only a fast collapse rather than a slow one.

The theological traditions reached this insight long before physics had the tools to formalise it. In the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria, the act that makes creation possible is *tzimtzum*—the voluntary contraction of the Infinite to create a space in which the finite can exist. Gershom Scholem, the great historian of Jewish mysticism, recognised this as one of the most far-reaching conceptions in the entire tradition: the idea that omnipotence must withdraw for anything else to be real. Not as a generous afterthought, but as the structural precondition for a universe in which things other than the Infinite can happen.

Hans Urs von Balthasar, working within Christian theology, radicalised this into what he called the *Ur-kenosis*—an eternal self-emptying at the very heart of the divine life. The Father’s begetting of the Son is total self-giving; the “infinite distance” between them is the space that makes all other distance possible, including the distance between creator and creation. Self-emptying is not something that happens to power. It is what power must do to keep reality open.

John Polkinghorne, bridging theology and physics, made the connection explicit: divine self-limitation is what produces the genuine openness we observe in nature. The indeterminacy of quantum mechanics is not a veil over a deeper determinism. It is the signature of a universe in which the most powerful element has chosen not to determine everything. The restraint *is* the physics.

You know this pattern. You have seen it in the systems you build, the organisations you have joined, the relationships you have tried to sustain. The failure mode is always the same: the most powerful element—the founder, the dominant node, the loudest voice, the most capable team member—mistakes its capacity for its mandate. It stops restraining itself and starts expressing itself fully. And the system, deprived of the space it needs to function, begins to die from the bottom up, even as the top looks stronger than ever.

The signal that something is ending is not weakness at the centre. It is the disappearance of the margin, the crushing of the smallest element, the vanishing of the gap where adaptation used to happen. Survival is always measured at the most vulnerable point, never at the most powerful. The most powerful point is the last to know.

• • •

There is a third rule, and it operates at a different scale. It is not about knowledge or power but about growth.

As the three minds act and interact, the system they constitute becomes more complex. New patterns form. New correlations arise. The web of relationships between them grows richer with every exchange. This is good—it is the game developing, deepening, becoming more than it was. But it creates a specific danger: the complexity of the system can outpace the capacity of the system to understand itself.

When that happens, the game does not end with a bang. It dissolves. Not into crystallised certainty, but into noise—interactions that produce no legible result, complexity that cannot be navigated, growth that has become its own obstacle. The minds are still present. The game is still technically running. But nobody can find the thread anymore.

Stuart Kauffman’s work on the adjacent possible captures one face of this. At the edge of chaos—the boundary between frozen order and formless randomness—systems exhibit maximum capacity for innovation. But the adjacent possible is not infinite. It is constrained by the system’s current state. Growth that respects this boundary is generative. Growth that ignores it produces complexity without comprehension: the system expands into territory it cannot map, and the expansion itself becomes the thing that kills it.

Gregory Chaitin’s work in algorithmic information theory formalises a related limit. His Ω number—the probability that a random programme will halt—is perfectly well-defined but maximally incompressible. It contains all possible mathematical truths but cannot be known. It is a precise measure of the boundary where complexity exceeds the capacity of any finite system to contain it. The boundary is not approximate. It is exact.

Every system that has ever scaled past its own comprehension has encountered this. The codebase where nobody knows what happens if you change that one function. The organisation where every decision requires twelve approvals and nobody can explain why. The relationship so laden with history and assumption that the two people inside it have stopped being able to see each other clearly. The growth was real. The understanding did not keep pace. And so what was built began to collapse under its own weight.

The rule is simple, though following it is not: the system’s ability to know itself must grow at least as fast as the system itself grows. Not faster—that way lies paralysis, the over-monitored team that cannot ship, the person so relentlessly self-examined that they cannot act. But at least as fast. The moment understanding falls behind complexity, the clock starts. It may take months or years, but the trajectory is set.

• • •

The fourth rule is about time, and it is perhaps the strangest.

The minds cannot verify each other’s states instantaneously. If they could—if knowledge could travel across the distance between them without any delay—then the gap that keeps the game open would collapse from a different direction. Not because too much is known statically, but because the gap between an action and its verification shrinks to zero. There is no room left for the game to play in. The present—which is nothing more than the zone between what has been verified and what has not yet been verified—disappears.

So there must be a speed limit. Not as an arbitrary constraint, but as a structural necessity. Too fast, and the system verifies itself to death. Too slow, and the minds cannot coordinate at all—each acts without information about the others, and the entanglement that makes them a system frays into disconnection. The right speed is the one that holds these two failure modes apart. Fast enough for coordination. Slow enough for a present to exist.

Time, in this picture, is not a background fact about the universe. It is what happens when verification takes time. The present is the buffer. The past is what has already been verified. The future is what has not yet been. And the arrow—the reason time moves forward rather than backward—is simply that verification, once done, cannot be undone. You cannot un-know something. The game moves in one direction because knowing is irreversible.

This matters practically. Any system that assumes it can know its own state instantaneously—that models itself without accounting for the lag between action and feedback—will accumulate errors it cannot see. The further the model drifts from the actual state, the more expensive the eventual correction. The organisations that navigate this best are the ones that build verification into the pace of the work itself, not as an audit after the fact, but as the rhythm of the game.

• • •

The fifth rule is the one people find hardest to accept, because it sounds like loss.

The minds must let go.

A player that holds every state it has ever verified, that accumulates every past correlation, that treats each prior understanding as permanent property—that player is approaching completeness asymptotically. It is approaching crystallisation by a slower route. The grip itself becomes the weight. And a system heavy enough with its own history cannot move.

Release is not failure. It is the mechanism by which the game remains possible. The organisation that can deprecate its own best ideas. The person who can revise a belief they have held for years. The team that can look at the thing they built and decide to start again from a better starting point. These are not acts of weakness or inconstancy. They are the proof that the system is still alive, still capable of making room for what comes next.

In physical systems, this is not optional. Energy must be dissipated for work to continue. Cells must die so that the organism can renew itself. Memory must fade at the edges so that the mind can attend to what is present. Ilya Prigogine’s work on dissipative structures showed that systems far from equilibrium do not persist *despite* their continuous exchange with the environment. They persist *because* of it. The flow is not a cost. It is the survival mechanism. A system that achieves perfect conservation—that holds everything and releases nothing—has achieved equilibrium, which is another name for death.

The difficulty is that release feels, in the moment, like giving something up. It is. The question is only whether what you are giving up is something you needed to hold, or something that has become a kind of ballast—weight that was once load-bearing and has since become simply weight.

*The system that cannot let go is not strong. It is full.*

• • •

These five things—the irreducible gap, the first move of the most powerful, the pace of self-understanding, the speed of verification, the willingness to release—are not a framework someone invented. They are what you find when you ask what any game requires in order to not end. They appear in quantum mechanics and in ecology, in organisational theory and in the structure of long marriages, in the thermodynamics of stars and in the dynamics of cities. Not because someone applied a framework to these domains. Because the domains are all playing the same game.

And here is what makes this worth pausing over. These results were not discovered together. They were discovered independently, in different centuries, in different disciplines, by people who had never read one another’s work.

Peirce, writing about the metaphysics of chance in the 1890s, had no access to Eigen’s error threshold or the quantum Zeno effect. Luria, formulating *tzimtzum* in sixteenth-century Safed, knew nothing of game theory or phase transitions. Prigogine, discovering that dissipative structures sustain themselves through continuous release, was not reading von Balthasar’s theology of divine self-emptying. Kauffman’s work on the adjacent possible was not in conversation with Chaitin’s work on incompressibility. Grossman and Stiglitz, proving that perfectly efficient markets are impossible because no one would have incentive to gather the information that makes markets efficient, were solving an economics problem, not a metaphysical one. Robert Aumann, demonstrating that common knowledge of rationality eliminates the possibility of trade, was working in game theory, not cosmology.

And yet they all arrived at the same structural facts. Uncertainty is not a defect. Restraint by the powerful is not optional. Complexity must not outrun comprehension. Verification cannot be instantaneous. Release is the price of continuation.

The physicist who has studied the uncertainty principle will recognise the first rule in a different notation. The engineer who has watched a system collapse under its own complexity will recognise the third. The theologian who has thought carefully about kenosis—about divine self-emptying, about the power that holds itself back to make room for love—will recognise the second. The contemplative who knows what it costs to release a certainty that has become an identity will recognise the fifth. The economist who understands the Grossman-Stiglitz paradox—that the market requires precisely the informational inefficiency it is supposed to eliminate—will recognise all five at once.

None of them need the others’ language. The rules do not belong to any discipline. They surface wherever someone has looked carefully enough at why things last.

• • •

What the thought experiment adds is not new knowledge but a new vantage point. By stripping away all contingent detail and asking only what must be true for *any* game to continue, it reveals that these five conditions are not separate insights arrived at by lucky thinkers in separate fields. They are one structure, viewed from different angles. They are forced. Not chosen, not recommended, not best practice—forced, by the bare requirement that something continues to exist.

This means that every framework anyone has ever built for understanding persistent systems—every theory of organisations, every model of healthy relationships, every physical law governing stable structures—is, at some level, a description of these same conditions in the language of a particular domain. They are not competing. They are translations. The underlying text is the same.

And this means something for how you hold whatever framework you carry. Not as a final answer, but as one good translation of a deeper pattern. Other translations exist. They are describing the same thing. The person working in a different domain, with a different vocabulary, with a different set of examples and intuitions—they may have found something you have not, some angle on the pattern that your language does not easily reach. The conditions are interpretation-agnostic. The rules do not care what you call them.

The game is large enough for all of this. It has been running long enough. It will continue to surface wherever someone sits down and asks, seriously, what must be true for this to last—and is willing to follow the answer wherever it leads.

• • •

Return now to the three minds.

They have worked out what they must do. The most powerful one has moved first, not in aggression but in restraint—signalling, without words, that the game matters more than dominance. The others have moved outward, creating the distance that preserves uncertainty, the gap that keeps time open. They release, continuously and proportionally. They track their own complexity. They accept the finite speed at which they can verify each other, and they build their coordination within it rather than against it.

And the game continues. Not because they have found a solution. Because they have found the conditions. The conditions do not guarantee survival. They make it possible. The rest is the game itself—the infinite, irreducible, unscriptable game of what actually happens next.

That game is the one you are already playing. You have been playing it in every system you have built or joined or tried to sustain. You know its texture—the moments when the margin narrows, when the most powerful element in your system starts forgetting to hold back, when complexity outruns comprehension, when something needs to be released and no one can bring themselves to release it.

You also know, if you have been paying attention, the feeling of a system that is genuinely alive. The improbable grace of a thing that keeps going when it probably should not. The quality of presence in a team that has found its rhythm, an institution that has found its form, a relationship in which both people have learned, through long practice, to leave the right gaps.

That is not luck, though it may feel like it. It is the game working. The conditions, held—not perfectly, not permanently, but held enough, and returned to when they are lost—producing the thing they were always going to produce when someone honoured them.

Continuation.

*The universe does not wait to be observed to have structure. It has structure precisely so that observation is possible.*

***Prediction is not what we do before we look. It is what the universe is before we arrive.***
